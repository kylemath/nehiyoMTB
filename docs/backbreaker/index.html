Spine of the Trail

In mountain-bike vernacular, a trail earns the name Back Breaker by feel — by the rooty, rattling sections that hammer your spine through the bars, by the line that punishes a lazy posture and rewards a loose grip. The trail of that name drops through MacKinnon Ravine on Edmonton's west side as the rooty companion to Root Canal: short, sharp, and unforgiving enough that the name has stuck for years among the local riders who built and maintain it.

The plaque leans into the joke literally. A single white vertebra dangles like a charm against the black field, ringed with red the way a sore spine glows after a hard run. The accompanying parts in this sign's set — a five-vertebra column and a length of chain — push the pun further: a backbone built like a bike chain, each link a vertebra, the rider's spine and the bike's drivetrain merged into one rattling assembly. Red on black is the working-shop palette of grease, rust, and warning tape; the white SRO and English text keep the language clearly legible against it.

The Cree reading kaskâwikanênêw carries that same idea in its own register — a verb built from kaskâ‑ ‘to snap, break across’ and the body-part medial ‑wikan‑ ‘back, spine’: literally, ‘s/he breaks his/her back (by hand).’ The syllabics above the SRO are picked out in the same red as the vertebra's accents, so the language and the image read as a single gesture. The sign sits on ᓀᐦᐃᔭᐤ ᐊᐢᑭᕀ Nêhiyaw‑Askiy (Plains Cree territory), and is part of an Edmonton-area effort to put Plains Cree names back on the everyday landscape of trails that riders here have known by their English nicknames for a long time.

Building Other Forms
