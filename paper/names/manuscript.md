::: {custom-style="Unindented"}
The mountain bike trails of Edmonton's river valley carry informal English names that riders pass on by word of mouth, and none of them is in Plains Cree, the Indigenous language of the territory the trails cross. This article describes a project that pairs {{nNames}} of these unofficial hodonyms with newly coined Plains Cree names on bilingual trailhead signs, each written in Standard Roman Orthography and in syllabics. Coding the English names by their source shows that most are borrowed from fauna, objects and popular culture rather than from the land itself. Coding the Cree names with Vermes's four operations for translating proper names shows that {{stratTranslationProper}} were translated word for word, {{stratModification}} were rebuilt as descriptions, and only {{stratTransference}} was carried over unchanged. Checking the two spellings of each name against each other shows that finished signs agree far more often than unreviewed candidates. I argue that translating playful informal names into an endangered language is mainly a decision about what to keep, and that this decision belongs with fluent speakers. The names are new coinages, not traditional Cree toponyms, and the article makes no claim to recover the latter.
:::

# Introduction

Anyone who rides the singletrack in Edmonton's river valley learns a second map of the city, made of trail names that appear on no street sign and no municipal map. *Raven*, *Machete*, *Six Shooter*, *Purgatory*, *Deliverance*, *Lovers Lane* and *Back Breaker* are names that riders pass on at trailheads and through crowd-sourced trail apps, and most of them began as a builder's joke or as a description of how a trail feels to ride. In onomastic terms they are unofficial hodonyms, names for routes, and because each names a short stretch of path within a park they are also microtoponyms. They are local knowledge in the plainest sense, but they are entirely English, and they lie within a territory that had Plains Cree names long before it had bicycles. The city itself is amiskwacîwâskahikan, 'Beaver Hills House', within Treaty 6, and Cree languages have more speakers in the Prairie provinces than any other Indigenous language [@statcan2023].

This article describes a project that set out to put Plains Cree beside those English names. {{alt|The project|The project, NehiyoMTB,}} makes a sign for each trail that keeps the English name riders already use, adds a Plains Cree name for it, and writes the Cree name twice, once in Standard Roman Orthography (SRO) and once in syllabics. The signs are 3D printed and fixed to trees at trail entrances, and {{nNames}} were finished at the time of writing. The English names are not mine: they were given by the volunteers who built the trails and by the riders who passed them on, and my part has been only the Cree names and the signs that carry them. It is equally important to be clear about what the Cree names are. They are new names, coined by translating or reworking the existing English names, and they are not traditional Cree names for these places. Work that restores Indigenous place names, such as the recovery of Anishinaabe toponyms on the White Earth Reservation [@pochedley2026], answers a different question and rests on a different kind of authority; nothing in this article should be read as a claim to have recovered a Cree name for any part of the valley.

What the project does offer is a small and well documented case of translating informal names into an Indigenous language, and that is the subject of this article. I ask three questions of it. First, where do the English trail names come from, in the sense of what they were named after? Second, what happens to each name when it is put into Cree: is it translated, rebuilt, replaced or simply carried over? Third, how consistent are the written forms, given that each Cree name has to be spelled twice in two systems that ought to agree? My answer, which I hold as a description of one project rather than a general finding, is that translating playful recreational names is mostly a matter of choosing what to keep, that the Cree names keep the reference of the English names far more often than their jokes, and that the two scripts provide a simple and useful check on whether a name has been looked at closely.

The rest of the article first places the project in work on unofficial recreational names, on Indigenous names in Canadian toponymy, on the translation of proper names, and on Plains Cree writing. It then describes the corpus, how the Cree names were made and reviewed, and how I coded them, before reporting the results and discussing what they suggest about who should name, and how.

## Unofficial names for recreational routes

Recreational routes are one of the few places where ordinary users still coin names freely, and onomastics has begun to take an interest in them. Helset's study of the names of climbing routes in Norway is the closest parallel to the present case [@helset2019]. Climbing routes, like mountain bike trails, are named by the people who first build or climb them, and Helset found that the 87 names he examined were motivated less by the route itself than by events on the first ascent, by a shared theme at a crag, and above all by analogy, which accounted for 51 of them. MacLennan & Childress study a different use of the phrase "trail names", the nicknames that long-distance hikers on the Appalachian Trail give one another, and show how such names follow group norms and do social work for the people who carry them [@maclennan2026]. The two studies agree that recreational naming is playful, socially bounded and largely invisible to official naming authorities, and the Edmonton trail names share all three features.

A typology of naming motives gives a way to describe that play. Tent & Blair built their typology for Australian placenames by asking what a name was meant to say about a place, and separated names that describe a feature, that commemorate a person or event, that are associated with something found there, and that are copied or borrowed from elsewhere [@tent2011]. Mountain bike trail names fit this scheme only loosely, because many of them point away from the place altogether, to a film, a song or a toy. For this reason I adapt the typology into a set of source domains suited to recreational names, as described under Methodology, while keeping its central question of what the name was given in reference to.

## Indigenous names in Canadian toponymy

Names of Indigenous origin have long been part of the Canadian map, and *Names* has followed them for decades. Rayburn's survey of geographical names of Amerindian origin in Canada traced how many such names entered official use through the filter of English and French spelling and reinterpretation [@rayburn1969; @rayburn1967]. Nicolaisen, writing about place-names in bilingual communities, showed that where two languages share a landscape, names may be translated, partially translated, adapted or left alone, and that the pattern of those choices reflects the relationship between the speech communities [@nicolaisen1975]. The present project inverts the usual direction of that process. Instead of an Indigenous name being taken into English, an English name is being taken into an Indigenous language, by a person who is not a member of the speech community, which raises questions of authority that the earlier literature did not need to ask.

Critical toponymy treats naming as an act of inscription that records who had the power to name [@roseredwood2010]. Tucker & Rose-Redwood's account of the Salish Sea shows that a new Indigenous-inflected name can be read both as recognition and as a symbolic gesture that leaves underlying arrangements untouched [@tucker2015], and Tuck & Yang's warning that decolonization is not a metaphor applies with force to a project of this size [@tuck2012]. Closer to home, Edmonton's twelve electoral wards received Indigenous names in 2020 through a committee guided by Knowledge Keepers and Elders [@edmontonwards2020], and trail projects in British Columbia have added Secwépemc and Nuu-chah-nulth names to trail signage under the leadership of the Nations concerned [@cumaata2023; @secwepemc_landmarks]. The Trans Canada Trail now invites trail groups to add Indigenous languages to wayfinding signs [@tct2023]. In each of these cases the Nation or a body of Knowledge Keepers led the naming, and that is the standard against which the present project should be measured.

## Translating proper names

Proper names are often said to be untranslatable because they refer rather than describe, but informal trail names are usually transparent descriptions or allusions, and they are translated routinely. Vermes proposes that translators of proper names choose among four operations [@vermes2003]. Transference carries the name over unchanged or only adapted in spelling; translation proper replaces it with a target-language expression of the same meaning; substitution replaces it with a different name or expression that has a comparable function in the target culture; and modification alters the name in a way that keeps some but not all of its meaning. The choice depends on what the translator takes to be important in the name, whether its reference, its meaning or its connotations. This framework is attractive here because trail names carry all three, and because it asks directly which of them survives.

## Plains Cree and its two scripts

Plains Cree, or nēhiyawēwin, is written in two systems that map onto each other almost one to one [@okimasis2008]. SRO uses the Latin alphabet and marks long vowels with a circumflex or macron, so that *kiwâpamitin* 'I see you' distinguishes *â* from *a*. Syllabics use one symbol for each consonant–vowel syllable, rotate it to change the vowel, add a dot to mark length and use small finals for syllable-final consonants, so the same word is ᑭᐚᐸᒥᑎᐣ. The western convention used for Plains Cree places the dot for *w* after the syllable it modifies, while eastern conventions place it before, and Unicode encodes both forms as separate characters.^1^ The Plains Cree dictionary itwêwina and the morphological modelling behind it [@itwewina; @harrigan2017], together with Wolvengrey's dictionary [@wolvengrey2001], were the main references for checking forms in this project, but a dictionary entry is not a name, and a good Cree name for an English pun is a creative and cultural choice rather than a lookup.

# Methodology

## Corpus and setting

The corpus is the set of trail names in the project's open catalogue, which records for each sign its English name, its Cree name in both scripts, a literal gloss and an interpretive note on how the name was built.^2^ At the time of analysis the catalogue held {{nModels}} sign records, of which {{nNames}} are finished signs that have been printed and photographed and the rest are draft pages. Figure 1 shows where the named trails lie: the signed network spans roughly {{mapExtentEW}} km east to west and {{mapExtentNS}} km north to south, almost all of it within the North Saskatchewan River valley and its ravines. A separate working list, which I call the workbench, holds three candidate Cree names for each of {{wbTrails}} trails in {{wbAreas}} riding areas, {{wbCands}} candidates in all, and serves as the pool from which sign names are chosen. I did not coin or change any of the English names. They were taken as they appear on Trailforks, the crowd-sourced trail database most riders in the city use, and they may differ in small ways from the names riders say aloud.

## How the Cree names were made

Every Cree name began as one of several candidates for the existing English name. Each candidate in the workbench records syllabics, SRO, an English gloss, a short note on how the name was built from its parts, and a note on whether and how it carries the English name's connotations or joke. **[k to supply: how the workbench candidates were produced. The repository suggests they were drafted with a large language model and then curated by you; if so, say which tool, when, and how you selected among candidates.]** Candidates were checked against itwêwina and Wolvengrey's dictionary [@itwewina; @wolvengrey2001] before a name was chosen for a sign. The workbench therefore represents machine-assisted first drafts, and the finished signs represent names that went through further rounds of human attention; the difference between the two is part of what the orthographic check below measures.

## Coding the names

I coded each of the {{nNames}} finished names in two ways. The first coding records the source domain of the English name, meaning what the name was given in reference to, adapted from Tent & Blair's question of motivation [@tent2011]. Seven domains emerged: fauna, objects, popular culture and brands, romance and innuendo, ride evaluation (names that describe how a trail feels, such as *Back Breaker*), people and roles, and phrases or puns. The second coding records the translation operation used for the Cree name, using Vermes's four operations [@vermes2003], with a fifth category for names that combine two operations. I assigned a name to translation proper when the Cree expression keeps the meaning of the English words, to modification when it keeps the reference but rebuilds the description (for example by describing an object for which Cree has no single word), to substitution when it replaces the English with an image of different content, and to transference when it carries the English name over in spelling only.

These codings were made by me alone, from the glosses and notes in the catalogue, and they should be read as provisional. A single coder who is not a Cree speaker can misread how much meaning a Cree expression keeps, and the coding table is published in full as supplementary material so that others, and in particular speakers, can check and correct it.^3^

## Checking the two spellings against each other

Because every name is written in two scripts, one kind of error can be checked automatically. I read each syllabic spelling back into SRO using the Unicode character names of the syllabics block, then compared the result with the SRO spelling recorded beside it, ignoring spaces, hyphens and case. Each pair was classed as agreeing exactly, differing only in vowel length (a length mark present in one script and absent from the other), or differing in letters. Separately, I flagged any spelling that uses a character outside the western Plains Cree conventions, most often a *w*-dot placed on the eastern side. This is a check of internal consistency only. A name can be spelled consistently in both scripts and still be wrong, and judging that is the work of speakers, not of a script. The same check was run on the finished signs, on the draft pages and on all workbench candidates.

# Results

## Where the English names come from

The English names draw on a narrow set of sources, and very few of them describe the land. Table 1 cross-tabulates source domain against translation strategy. Fauna, objects and popular culture account for {{domFauna}}, {{domObject}} and {{domPopularCultureAndBrands}} names respectively, together {{nTopThree}} of the {{nNames}}. Fauna names such as *Raven*, *Porcupine* and *Howl* are the closest the set comes to describing a place, since they presumably record animals seen or heard nearby, but the catalogue does not record that history, and I do not assume it. Object names such as *Machete*, *Six Shooter* and *Ray Gun* describe the experience of riding rather than the trail's surroundings, while popular culture names such as *Purple Haze*, *Wonder Woman* and *Deliverance* point away from the place entirely, to a song, a superhero and a film. Two names refer to romance or innuendo, two evaluate the ride (*Back Breaker*, *Purgatory*), and one is a phrase with a pun in it (*I See You*, which its builder, an intensive-care physician, chose because it sounds like "ICU").

This pattern is close to what Helset found for climbing routes, where most names were motivated by analogy and themes rather than by features of the route [@helset2019]. It also differs sharply from the motivations that dominate official toponymy, in which descriptive and commemorative names are common [@tent2011]. ## How the names were translated

Table 2 lists the finished names in the approximate order in which they were made, with their Cree forms, literal senses and the strategy I assigned. Translation proper was the most common operation, used for {{stratTranslationProper}} names ({{stratTranslationProperPct}}). Some of these are single dictionary words, as with *kahkakiw* 'raven' and *kâkwa* 'porcupine'; others translate a phrase word for word, as with *mamahtâw iskwew* 'wondrous woman' for *Wonder Woman*, or render an English noun phrase as a Cree verb, as with *kaskâwikanênêw* 's/he breaks his/her back' for *Back Breaker*. In several cases translation proper keeps the meaning of the words while losing the allusion: *paspîhiwêwin* 'the rescuing of people' translates *Deliverance* as an abstract noun and leaves the film behind, and *kiwâpamitin* 'I see you' keeps the phrase but cannot keep the pun on "ICU".

Modification accounted for {{stratModification}} names ({{stratModificationPct}}), and it clusters among the object names, where Cree has no established single word for the thing. *Machete* became *mistâpiskihkomân* 'great iron knife', *Ray Gun* became *sâpwâpahcikan pâskisikan* 'through-shooting firearm', and *Bed Springs* became a short descriptive phrase meaning 'a bed, the things that bounce'. *Purgatory*, a theological term without a direct Cree equivalent, became *kâsenamâkêw iskotêw*, which the sign's page glosses as 'the fire that refuses (people)'. Substitution was used once, for *Purple Haze*, where the Cree *nîpâmâyâtân pekiseyâhk* 'our way is hazy, we ride through the haze' drops the color and the song and substitutes an image of riding. Transference was used once, for *Slinky*, a brand name kept as it is and spelled into syllabics, and one name, *Victoria's Secret*, mixes the two, keeping the personal name *Victoria* in roman letters and translating the rest as a verb meaning 'she keeps it secret from her'.

The Cree names are also longer and more often built from parts than the English. Of the {{nNames}} names, {{nMultiWord}} are written as two or more words in SRO, and {{nDiminutive}} ({{nDiminutivePct}}) end in a diminutive suffix, among them {{diminutiveNames}}. The diminutive often does work that the English name does by other means, turning a lane into *mêskanâs* 'little path' or a sled into *capanaskos* 'little toboggan', and it gives several of the names a fond tone that the English lacks.

# Discussion

## What translation keeps

The clearest result is that the Cree names keep the reference of the English names much more often than their play. Translation proper and modification together account for {{nRefKept}} of the {{nNames}} names, and in both the Cree tells a reader what the trail is called after, whether a raven, a knife or a sled. What is usually lost is the second layer: the allusion to a film, a song or a brand, the slang sense of *Silverfox*, and the pun in *I See You*. This is not surprising, since those layers depend on English-language popular culture, but it changes what the names are. An English trail name like *Deliverance* works as an in-joke for riders who know the film; its Cree counterpart works as an ordinary word that a learner can look up and use. In Nicolaisen's terms, the two names on each sign are not equivalents in a bilingual community but a pairing made for learners, in which the Cree name is closer to a description than to a nickname [@nicolaisen1975].

That shift may be a good thing for the purpose of the signs. A rider who sees *kahkakiw* and ᑲᐦᑲᑭᐤ beside *Raven* every week learns a common noun and two ways of writing it, attached to a place and a habit. A literal rendering of a film title would teach much less. The cases of modification suggest something further, because names such as *mistâpiskihkomân* show a rider how Cree builds a word for a new thing out of existing parts. I offer these as possibilities that the project was designed around, not as findings, since I have not measured what riders learn.

## Humour, innuendo and respect

Trail names are often jokes, and some are crude, and that is where translation is most difficult. A literal Cree rendering of a double entendre can put a language that many people hold as sacred in the service of a joke it was never part of. Among the finished signs the two names coded as romance or innuendo were handled in a gentle way: *Lovers Lane* became *nicimos mêskanâs* 'my sweetheart's little path', and *Farmer's Daughter* became a plain possessive phrase that drops the genre of joke the English evokes. The workbench notes show that the same question arose for many more trails. One option that the coding scheme does not show, because it leaves no sign behind, is not to give a trail a Cree name at all. A trail whose English name cannot be carried into Cree with dignity should probably keep its English name alone, and the people best placed to make that call are speakers and Elders, not the person who printed the sign. **[k to supply: any trail names that were declined, or changed, for this reason.]**

## Who names

The project began the other way around from the Indigenous naming initiatives cited above. In those cases a Nation or a committee of Knowledge Keepers led the naming and a trail organization or a city supplied the infrastructure [@edmontonwards2020; @cumaata2023; @secwepemc_landmarks]. Here, an individual rider made the signs first and sought review afterwards. That order is the main risk in the project, and it is sharpened by the use of machine-assisted drafting, which makes candidate names cheap and shifts the work to deciding which are right. Studies of language technology for Indigenous languages warn that tools built without communities tend to treat languages as data rather than as the practices of people who should set the agenda [@mager2023; @bird2020]. The orthographic results give a concrete picture of that warning: names that went through repeated human review are consistent, and unreviewed candidates are not. Putting Cree names on trails does not return land or decision-making to anyone [@tuck2012], and the project should claim no more than to make a language more visible on a small network of paths, under the guidance of the people whose language it is.

## Limitations

This is a description of one project, with a small corpus and a single coder, and its claims should be read that way. The coding of source domains relies on what the names refer to, not on what their builders intended, which the catalogue does not record. The coding of translation strategies relies on English glosses, and a speaker may judge that a name I coded as translation proper in fact changes its meaning, or the reverse. The orthographic check measures consistency between two spellings and says nothing about whether either is good Cree. The article does not report what riders think of the names or whether they learn from them, which would require a separate study with ethics review and riders' consent.

# Conclusion

The English names of Edmonton's mountain bike trails are a repertoire of jokes, animals, objects and borrowed titles that riders share as local knowledge. Translating {{nNames}} of them into Plains Cree for bilingual signs showed that most could be translated or rebuilt so that they keep what they refer to, while their allusions and puns were mostly lost. The Cree names that resulted are new coinages, not traditional place names, and their value lies in placing ordinary Cree words, in both of the language's scripts, where riders see them again and again. Writing each name twice gave a simple check that separates names which have been looked at closely from names which have not. The most important next step is not technical: it is a more formal relationship with Cree speakers, Elders and language organizations in Edmonton, so that choosing and correcting names is a standing practice with clear credit rather than a series of favours.
